# PART 6: PRACTICAL MATTERS

# 19. Safety, Health, Connectivity and Packing

Most Dolomites trips go smoothly. The problems that do occur are usually small and avoidable: weather that turns, a hike that is too long, a phone with no signal, a missing insurance cover. This chapter shows you how to prepare so that a small problem stays small.

## Mountain safety: ten rules

1. **Check the forecast the night before and again in the morning.** Mountain weather differs from valley weather.
2. **Start early.** Aim to finish by early afternoon in July and August.
3. **Set a turnaround time** and stick to it.
4. **Match the hike to the weakest person** in your group.
5. **Check the last lift or bus back** before you set off.
6. **Carry layers.** The temperature drops by about 6 °C for every 1,000 metres you climb.
7. **Carry water, food, a first-aid kit, a headlamp and a charged phone.**
8. **Stay on marked paths.** Note the trail number at each junction.
9. **Tell someone your route** and expected return time.
10. **Turn back when in doubt.** Cloud, snow patches, wet rock and a late start all raise the risk.

## Weather and thunderstorms

### Forecast sources

- **South Tyrol:** the provincial weather service (Landeswetterdienst) publishes forecasts and a mountain bulletin.
- **Trentino:** Meteotrentino.
- **Veneto:** the regional agency ARPAV issues the "Dolomiti Meteo" bulletin, prepared by the avalanche centre in Arabba.
- **Combined:** the MeteoDolomiti portal covers Belluno, Trento and Bolzano.

Use more than one source. The links are in Appendix C.

### Thunderstorm rules

Afternoon thunderstorms are common in July and August. They can build quickly, bringing heavy rain, hail and lightning.

- **Leave ridges, summits and exposed places** at the first sign of building clouds or distant thunder.
- **Move to a hut, a sturdy building or a car.**
- **Keep away from cables and metal.** Do not use a via ferrata in a thunderstorm.
- **Do not wait to see if it passes.** Plan to be low or in shelter before the afternoon.

## Emergencies

| Need | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Any emergency** | Call **112**. It is free and works without a SIM card, even if the phone is blocked or out of credit. In South Tyrol and Trentino, 112 is the single emergency number. |
| **Mountain rescue** | Call 112. The dispatcher will alert mountain rescue and decide on a helicopter. |
| **App** | Download the free **Where ARE U** app before you travel. It sends your GPS position to the emergency centre. It needs a signal. |
| **What to say** | Who you are, where you are (trail number, nearest hut or lift), what happened, how many people are hurt, and the weather. Stay on the line. |
| **Hospital** | Go to the *Pronto Soccorso* (emergency department) of the nearest hospital. |
| **Pharmacy** | Look for a green cross (*farmacia*). A rota list on the door shows which one is open at night. |

### Rescue costs

Helicopter rescue is billed separately. In South Tyrol, the average cost reported was about €4,000, and one operation of 188 flight minutes cost €26,320. Advice from local sources is to hold insurance that covers at least €10,000 for rescue and recovery.

## Travel insurance

Check that your policy covers:

- **Mountain rescue and helicopter evacuation.** Check that search, rescue and recovery costs are covered, including helicopter flights, and what the limit is.
- **Medical treatment and repatriation.**
- **The activities you plan.** Some policies exclude via ferrata, climbing, or walking above a set altitude. Read the limits.
- **Trip cancellation and delays.**
- **Baggage and equipment.**

**Health cards.** EU citizens with a European Health Insurance Card, and UK citizens with a Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC), receive emergency care on the same terms as residents. The card does not cover everything, and you may have to pay upfront and claim back. Travel insurance is still essential. Visitors from the United States and elsewhere have no such card and should check how their own health insurance works abroad.

## Health considerations

### Altitude

- **Above about 2,500 m,** headache, dizziness, nausea, tiredness and poor sleep can occur, especially after a fast ascent by cable car. Examples in this book: Piz Boè from Sass Pordoi (2,950 m at the start) and the Marmolada (up to 3,265 m).
- **If you feel unwell, go down.** Do not push on.
- **Children** cannot always explain how they feel. A child who becomes unwell above 2,500 m should be taken to lower altitude.
- **Heart or lung conditions and older adults.** Talk to your doctor before a trip with high lifts and long climbs. Plan time to adjust before going very high.

### Sun

Ultraviolet exposure rises by about 10 to 12 percent for every 1,000 metres of altitude. Use sunscreen (SPF 30 to 50), a hat and sunglasses, and reapply often. Cool air hides the effect.

### Ticks

Ticks live in damp, warm, shady woodland, mostly up to about 900 m. They are found in many South Tyrol valleys, including around Bolzano and parts of the Val Pusteria and Valle Isarco. They can carry Lyme disease and tick-borne encephalitis (TBE).

- **Wear long trousers and closed shoes** in woods and tall grass.
- **Check your skin** after walks, and remove any tick promptly.
- **Ask your doctor about TBE vaccination** if you will spend a lot of time outdoors in risk areas. It needs time to build protection, so plan weeks ahead.
- **Watch for a red circular rash** in the weeks after a bite.

### Water and food

Tap water in the valleys is safe to drink. In huts, ask whether water from taps or fountains is drinking water. Carry enough for the day.

## Connectivity

### Mobile coverage

- **Villages and resorts** such as Cortina, Val Gardena and Alta Badia have good coverage.
- **Above the tree line,** on trails and via ferrata, signal is patchy. One guide put coverage at about 80 percent of the time while hiking.
- **Many huts have Wi-Fi.** Do not rely on it.
- **Mountains block signals,** so a poor signal in a valley bottom does not mean the network is down.

### Staying connected

- **EU residents** can usually use their home plan at no extra cost. Check your plan's rules.
- **Visitors from outside the EU:** compare roaming, a local SIM and an eSIM. Check the plan's data allowance and activation before you travel.
- **Download offline maps** (for example Google Maps or Maps.me) before you leave the valley.
- **Carry a power bank.** Cold drains batteries quickly.
- **Consider a satellite messenger** if you will be in remote areas for several days.

## Money

- **Currency:** euro.
- **Cards** work in hotels, shops and most restaurants. **Carry cash** for huts, parking machines and small purchases.
- **ATMs** are in the larger villages, not at huts.
- **Tourist tax** is charged per person per night by your accommodation (Chapter 5).
- **Tax-free shopping.** Non-EU visitors can claim a VAT refund on purchases of at least €70.01 in a single shop on one day (including VAT). For purchases from 1 January 2026, travellers have six months to return the customs-stamped invoice. A simplified passport-based validation system was planned from 1 July 2026. Check the current procedure at the airport.
- **Tipping:** see Chapter 16.

## Packing lists

### Summer day pack

- Waterproof jacket and trousers or a poncho
- Warm layer (fleece or light down)
- Hat, gloves, sunglasses, sunscreen (SPF 30 to 50), lip balm
- Two litres of water, snacks, lunch if not eating at a hut
- First-aid kit, blister plasters, any medication
- Headlamp (essential for tunnels and early starts)
- Map or offline maps, phone, power bank
- Cash (euros), ID, insurance details
- Walking poles (optional)
- Small bag for litter

### For the trip

- **Footwear:** sturdy hiking boots with ankle support and a good sole, plus walking sandals or trainers for the evening.
- **Clothing:** quick-dry layers, long trousers, a light rain jacket, swimwear for spas.
- **Documents:** passport, travel insurance, EHIC or GHIC, driving licence (and International Driving Permit if needed), booking confirmations (printed copies of hut bookings).
- **Electronics:** Italy uses 230 V with two-pin plugs, so bring an adapter if needed.
- **Hut stays:** sleeping bag liner, towel, indoor shoes, earplugs, headlamp, a small amount of cash.
- **Via ferrata:** helmet, harness, lanyard with energy absorber, gloves. Or rent them (Chapter 14).

### Winter

- Insulated ski jacket and trousers, base layers, goggles and sunglasses
- Helmet (ski rental shops provide them), gloves, buff or neck gaiter
- Après-ski boots with grip
- Sun cream and lip balm (the sun is strong on snow)
- Ski pass, chargers and a power bank (cold drains batteries)
- Winter tyres or chains in the car (Chapter 4)

## Accessibility, families and older travellers

### Accessibility

- **Alpe di Siusi.** The cable car from Siusi has spacious cabins for up to 16 people, with priority boarding for wheelchair users. Accessible toilets are at the lift stations and in Castelrotto and Siusi. A flat, barrier-free walk runs from the top cable car station to Hotel Sole. People with a European Disability Card could drive to Compatsch at any time with prior registration in 2026.
- **Dolomiti Accessibili.** A regional initiative lists accessible itineraries and viewpoints, with 36 routes across Veneto, Trentino and South Tyrol.
- **Check each lift.** Not all cable cars suit wheelchairs or mobility aids. Ask the operator before you travel.

### Children

- **Choose short, flat walks** with a hut or playground as the goal (Chapter 14).
- **Lagazuoi tunnels** are not suited to children under 8.
- **Watch for altitude symptoms** above 2,500 m.
- **Pack layers.** Children cool down fast.

### Older travellers

- **Use lifts** to avoid long climbs.
- **Plan rest days** after high or long days.
- **Carry medication** and a list of your conditions.
- **See your doctor** before travel if you have heart or lung problems.

## Common mistakes

- Starting a walk in good weather and not checking the afternoon forecast.
- Relying on a phone signal for navigation.
- Travelling without insurance that covers mountain rescue.
- Carrying no cash.
- Underestimating sun, cold and altitude.
- Missing the last lift or bus.

1. Buy insurance that covers mountain rescue and the activities you plan.
