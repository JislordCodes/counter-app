# Welcome to the Dolomites

Pale limestone towers that glow pink at sunset. Alpine meadows that roll out beneath vertical walls. Mountain huts that serve hot dumplings and strudel at 2,500 metres. Villages where people greet you in Italian, German or Ladin, sometimes all three in one sentence. This is the Dolomites, and it is one of the most rewarding mountain regions in Europe to visit.

The Dolomites sit in the far north-east of Italy, spread across the provinces of South Tyrol (Alto Adige), Trentino and Belluno. In 2009 UNESCO inscribed nine mountain systems here on the World Heritage List for their striking scenery and their importance to the study of geology. The rock is dolomite, a carbonate mineral that gives the peaks their pale colour and their sheer, fortress-like shapes. The highest summit is the Marmolada, at 3,343 metres.

## Why a 2027 edition?

Travel to the Dolomites changed quickly in the mid-2020s. Several of the best-known places now work on **advance booking, timed entry or vehicle restrictions**, especially in July and August. Examples include the lake at Braies, the toll road to the Tre Cime di Lavaredo, and the cable car to Seceda. Rules, prices and timetables differ from year to year, and they are often announced only a few months ahead.

This guide is written to help you plan well under those conditions. It explains how the region works, what to book early, what you can safely leave open, and how to choose a base. It also gives you routes and itineraries you can adapt.

## How this guide handles prices and dates

Many tickets, timetables and access rules for 2027 will not be published until the spring of that year. Where this book gives a price or a rule, it tells you which season it comes from, and you should treat it as a **guide to the likely level**, not a promise. Official websites are the last word. Chapter 3 lists what to check and when.

## How the book is organised

- **Part 1: Planning.** When to go, what to book, how to get there and around, and where to stay.
- **Part 2: What to see and do.** The headline sights, the best hikes, via ferrate, cycling and road passes, and winter in the mountains.
- **Part 3: Living like a local.** Food and drink, the three cultures of the region, and sample itineraries.
- **Part 4: Practical matters.** Safety, packing, money, language and responsible travel.

## Names and languages

Almost every place has two or three names. The South Tyrol side is mostly German- and Italian-speaking, with Ladin spoken in the Val Gardena, Val Badia and Val di Fassa areas. Road signs show several names, and so do maps and websites. This guide uses the name you are most likely to see on a map or booking site, and gives alternatives on first mention.

- Alpe di Siusi / Seiser Alm
- Ortisei / St. Ulrich / Urtijëi
- Lago di Braies / Pragser Wildsee
- Tre Cime di Lavaredo / Drei Zinnen
- Bolzano / Bozen
- Bressanone / Brixen
- Val Gardena / Gröden
- Alta Badia / Hochabtei

## A note on safety and responsibility

Mountains are beautiful and unforgiving. Weather can change within an hour. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common above the tree line. Many of the walks in this book are suitable for ordinary fit walkers, but some include exposed sections or fixed cables, and via ferrate need proper equipment and skills. Read Chapter 13 before you set out, and use your own judgement on the day.
